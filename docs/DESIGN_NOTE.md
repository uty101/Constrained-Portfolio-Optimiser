# Design note: why each constraint is there

A portfolio implementation desk turns a model's target weights into orders a fund can actually trade. Every rule below exists on such a desk for a reason that has little to do with the model, and each paragraph says what this repo's outputs showed about it. Figures come from `outputs/tables/`, `outputs/results/` and the demo in [cli_demo.md](cli_demo.md).

**The 30% position cap.** Mandates limit how much of the fund can sit in 1 holding, so that 1 bad call cannot sink it and so that the fund never owns too large a share of a thinly traded line. Here the cap does a second job: it stops mean-variance from piling into whichever asset had the best 36 months. The capped mean-variance fund held 4.6 positions on average and spent long stretches with assets pinned at 30% (Chart 2), which is the optimiser telling you it would have gone further if allowed.

**Long only.** Most mandates forbid shorts because short positions need borrow, margin and a risk team willing to own unlimited losses. The backtest shows what happens without that rule: the budget-only fund on the sample covariance ran gross exposure between 27 and 129 times the fund, and 3 of the 4 budget-only funds lost everything. No long-only fund in the backtest was ruined.

**The full-investment budget.** The weights sum to 1, with no cash line, because the fund is sold as an allocation to these assets and the client holds its own cash. The cost is that a low-risk allocator cannot choose a higher risk level by gearing. Risk parity, minimum variance and HRP all ran at a fraction of equal weight's volatility, which is why `levered_risk_based.csv` scales them up to compare like with like. In the demo, the 5% of the book in cash is invested because the budget requires it.

**The turnover limit.** A turnover budget caps how much of the fund trades each month. It protects against an optimiser that chases noise, and it keeps orders within what the desk can execute without moving prices. At τ = 0.30 the limit bound in 62 months. In this backtest, every binding limit gave up more ex-ante expected return than it saved in modelled cost, and the realised effect cannot be told apart from 0. The limit earns its place as a guard on execution and on model error rather than as a source of return.

**Flat basis-point costs in the objective.** Putting cost into the objective makes the optimiser trade only when the expected gain beats the spread. With costs scaled to 0, 1 and 3 times their base level, the capped mean-variance fund's mean turnover fell from 0.22 to 0.18 to 0.12, while its Sharpe ratio barely moved. Flat costs ignore market impact, so the objective understates the cost of large trades; for a $10m book in these ETFs that is a small error.

**The feasibility relaxation.** A drifted weight can sit above its cap. If the turnover budget is too small to bring it back, the problem has no solution, so the rule solves with the smallest turnover that restores the cap, plus 1e-6, and records that the limit was relaxed. The ordering is deliberate: a mandate breach outranks a turnover budget, because a breach is a compliance event and an overspent budget is not. The demo book holds GLD at 40% against a 30% cap, and merely reaching the feasible set takes turnover of 0.2501. At τ = 0.05 the backtest relaxed the limit in 4 months.

**Lot rounding and minimum notional.** Exchanges trade whole shares, and a desk will not send an order whose ticket cost exceeds its benefit. Trades are rounded toward the target, so rounding never overshoots, and orders under $1,000 are dropped. In the demo, rounding moved turnover from 0.300000 to 0.299855 and left no weight further than 0.00012 from its target.

**The cash repair.** Estimated costs come out of cash, so a trade list that spends every dollar on assets leaves cash below 0. The repair cuts the largest buy 1 lot at a time until cash is at least 0. In the demo it cut the XLK buy by 6 shares and left $80.52 of cash, which is cheaper than a margin call.

**The solver fallback and hold rule.** A production run cannot stop because 1 month's optimisation failed. CLARABEL is tried first; if it does not report `optimal`, SCS gets 1 retry; if that fails too, the fund holds its drifted weights and the month is flagged. Over the 26 strategies, SCS was called 873 times and the hold rule never fired. Holding is the safe default because it trades nothing, so a solver failure costs some tracking but no trading cost.

**Blocking set A in the CLI.** `pctrade` refuses budget-only strategies, because they short and gear without limit and are research objects, not tradable funds. The backtest is the evidence: 3 of the 4 went to 0, and the survivor fell 98% from peak to trough. A tool that turns targets into orders should not be able to send those.
